\begin{abstract}
Federated learning (FL) models deployed in production face concept drift:
the data distribution shifts after deployment, and model quality degrades
until a retraining action is taken. In real deployments the shift is often
gradual: the fraction of corrupted inputs rises slowly over a transition
interval whose duration is the drift width used in the drift-detection
literature. We study reactive retraining in synchronous FL as the drift
transition lengthens from abrupt to gradual. Each client monitors its
unlabeled input stream with a local
uncertainty-based detector (Monte Carlo dropout, Shannon entropy, and an
ADWIN adaptive-window test); the server aggregates the local alarms over a
short tick window and, at most once, triggers a single global retraining
round executed on each client's current mixture. The primary metric is the
service downtime, the cumulative virtual time in which the served-model
accuracy stays below a quality threshold. In 60 paired episodes (four
calibrated corruptions, five seeds, three ramp durations of 50--200\,s)
over a fixed 600\,s horizon, compared with a paired baseline that never
retrains, detection succeeds in 59 of 60 episodes (14 of 15 in the
Gaussian-noise scenario); the detection delay grows with the ramp duration
for the three visual corruptions (98--140\,s) and reaches 256\,s for
Gaussian noise; the reactive policy reduces the mean downtime from
408--562\,s in the baseline to 0--178\,s, reaching zero downtime at
$\tau=0.5$ in the longest-ramp fog scenario; and long ramps preserve
clean-set accuracy for the visual corruptions (1--2 points loss instead of
9--21). Within the
exercised regime (CIFAR-10, IID partition, ten clients, a single drift and
at most one retraining round), a reactive label-free policy is effective
and less destructive to previously learned knowledge.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{F}{ederated} learning (FL) trains a shared model from data that
remains distributed across clients \cite{mcmahan2017}. Once deployed, the
model is served in an environment whose data distribution can change; such
concept drift degrades the served model's quality until a retraining action
is taken, and detecting it---usually without access to labels at inference
time---is a prerequisite for triggering that action. A common simplification
in the literature is to inject drift abruptly: the distribution switches at
a single instant. In practice, however, the shift is often gradual: new
data appears mixed with old data, and the proportion of shifted inputs
grows slowly over a transition interval whose duration is the drift width
used in the drift-detection literature
\cite{poenaru2022,cerqueira2026}. We model this continuum as a linear ramp
of the corrupted-data fraction from zero to one over a configurable number
of monitoring ticks \cite{cerqueira2026}; Casado et
al.~\cite{casado2022} define gradual drift as the case in which the new
concept appears mixed with the old one. Our grid spans short ramps
(equivalent to the abrupt regime) and a long ramp in which the detector
operates mid-transition, covering the boundary between the two regimes.

Our reactive policy follows the architecture that the FL drift literature
converged to: \emph{local detection, central decision}
\cite{jothimurugesan2023,chow2023,mavromatis2024,patel2026,han2026,fu2025,
zhou2026,casado2022,panchal2023,chen2026}. Each client runs an uncertainty
drift
detector (UDD) \cite{baier2023}: Monte Carlo (MC) dropout \cite{gal2016}
over $T$ stochastic forwards, mean Shannon entropy, and an ADWIN adaptive
window test \cite{bifet2007} per client.
The server collects the per-client alarms over a sliding window of two
monitoring ticks and triggers when the fraction of distinct flagged clients
reaches a quorum. The triggered action is a single global retraining round
on the mixture each client currently serves, in line with the explicit
retraining budgets of DriftGuard \cite{han2026} and ShiftEx
\cite{bhope2025} and Modyn's trigger/data-selection separation
\cite{bother2025}.

We evaluate the policy under an operational lens. The primary metric is the
service \emph{downtime}: the cumulative virtual time in which the corrupted
accuracy of the served model stays below a quality threshold $\tau$,
computed as a stepwise integral without interpolation over a fixed horizon.
To our knowledge, no work in the reviewed FL drift literature quantifies
the time a served model stays below a quality threshold; we detail this
gap in Section II-E.

Our contributions are: (i) a \textbf{service-downtime metric} for FL under
drift, computed as a stepwise integral over a fixed horizon; (ii) a
\textbf{collective quorum policy} aggregating per-client label-free alarms
over a short tick window, a mechanism absent from the reviewed literature,
closest to the participation gate of FL-MalDrift \cite{patel2026}, assessed
descriptively (ablations against alternative triggers are future work);
(iii) a \textbf{federated installation of UDD}, with one
detector per client sharing the global model, extending the centralized
detector of Baier et al.~\cite{baier2023}; (iv) an \textbf{auditable
experimental infrastructure} that pairs the reactive arm with a
no-retraining baseline from the same checkpoint, random state, stream, and
horizon, and validates the pair before publication; and (v) an
\textbf{empirical characterization of gradual drift} over 60 paired
episodes.

Our main findings are: (i) detection succeeds in 59 of 60 episodes (14 of
15 in the Gaussian-noise scenario); (ii)
the detection delay grows with the ramp duration for the three visual
corruptions, while Gaussian noise exhibits larger
delays and dispersion; (iii) the reactive policy reduces the mean downtime
from 408--562\,s in the baseline to 0--178\,s, reaching zero downtime in
the longest-ramp fog scenario; and (iv) long ramps preserve clean-set
accuracy for the visual corruptions, because the retraining set retains a
substantial share of clean data.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\subsection{Concept drift in federated learning}
Concept drift is the change in the data distribution over time
\cite{criado2022,mahdi2025}; the taxonomy distinguishes virtual drift (only
$P(X)$ changes) from real drift (the conditional $P(Y\mid X)$ changes), and
sudden, gradual, and recurrent drifts \cite{mahdi2025}. Staggered drifts,
in which different clients drift at different times, are studied by
Jothimurugesan et al.~\cite{jothimurugesan2023}. Gradual drift, the regime
studied here, mixes the new concept with the old one with a growing
proportion over time \cite{casado2022}; the transition duration is the
drift width, the standard parameter controlling the speed of the change
\cite{poenaru2022,cerqueira2026}.

Our scope is deliberately delimited to drifts accompanied by a change in
$P(X)$ (covariate shift with preserved labels), the assumption under which
label-free detection is structurally possible: label-free methods are
deterministic functions of the inputs, so a change confined to $P(Y\mid X)$
produces no observable signal, a blind spot measured by Solozobov
\cite{solozobov2026} and declared by Baier et al.~\cite{baier2023}.

\subsection{Local detection, central decision}
A recurring finding in FL drift research is that drift detection must be
local. Jothimurugesan et al.~\cite{jothimurugesan2023} show that testing the
aggregated error at the server fails during staggered transitions, because
the errors of drifted clients and of clients that have not yet drifted
cancel each other out. FLARE \cite{chow2023}, FLAME \cite{mavromatis2024},
FL-MalDrift \cite{patel2026}, DriftGuard \cite{han2026}, FedDAA
\cite{fu2025}, FedPLC \cite{zhou2026}, the
label-free virtual-drift detection of FELK/FL-HVD \cite{chen2026}, and
Casado et al.~\cite{casado2022} follow local detection with central
decision: nine of the twelve concept-drift FL works we reviewed do so.
The three exceptions are informative: FLASH detects drift server-side
from the magnitude of aggregated parameter updates \cite{panchal2023},
Stallmann et al. use a global fuzzy-clustering detector
\cite{stallmann2024}, and Adaptive-FedAVG forgoes detection altogether,
adapting the server-side learning rate to raise the plasticity of the
learning phase, a passive alternative to local detection
\cite{canonaco2021}. Our design follows the consensus; the specific
signal, however, is label-free uncertainty (Section IV-A).

\subsection{Label-free drift detection}
The uncertainty drift detector (UDD) of Baier et al.~\cite{baier2023} is the
reference label-free method: for each input, the model performs $T$
stochastic forwards with dropout enabled (MC dropout, \cite{gal2016}); the
mean Shannon entropy feeds an ADWIN adaptive window test \cite{bifet2007}.
ADWIN is chosen over DDM/EDDM because these require binomial inputs,
whereas the entropy signal is not restricted to a binomial distribution
\cite{baier2023}. Lobo et al.~\cite{lobo2023} connect concept drift and
model uncertainty, and uncertainty estimators are known to degrade under
dataset shift---exactly the signal the detector exploits
\cite{ovadia2019}. The published UDD uses
$T=50$ forwards; our $T=5$ is a cost decision whose sensitivity is left as
future work.
D3M \cite{nguyen2025} is a complementary label-free method based on model
disagreement, with false-positive guarantees under the covariate-shift
assumption. PUDD \cite{lu2025} is complementary too: it tests the full
distribution of a prediction-uncertainty index with a Pearson chi-square
test whose significance level directly controls the false-positive rate,
unlike UDD's mean-tracking ADWIN stage.

\subsection{Retraining orchestration and budgets}
DriftGuard \cite{han2026} formalizes the retraining decision as a tuple
$\pi^t=(\mathrm{Trig},S,\theta)$: whether to retrain, which devices
participate, and which parameters to update, reporting retraining-cost
reductions of 80--83\%. Modyn \cite{bother2025} separates trigger policies
(time, volume, performance, drift) from data-selection policies; its drift
trigger uses MMD (with KS among candidate statistics) and an adaptive
percentile threshold. In the reviewed
FL works, only DriftGuard \cite{han2026} and ShiftEx \cite{bhope2025} make
the retraining budget an explicit parameter (a named quantity controlling
the number of post-trigger training rounds). We adopt an explicit budget of
a single global round and retrain on the distribution each client currently
serves.

\subsection{Metrics gap}
The drift-detection benchmark of Cerqueira et al.~\cite{cerqueira2026}
documents the lack of standardized temporal metrics (normalized detection
delay, alarm rate, acceptance windows), as do FL drift surveys
\cite{mahdi2025}. Detection latency \cite{baier2023,chow2023},
retraining duration, and cost per retraining \cite{han2026} are reported
separately; none of the works we reviewed quantifies the time the served
model stays below a quality threshold, the downtime metric we adopt, which
mirrors the SLO practice of MLOps. Downtime language exists in adjacent
ML-systems work---federated unlearning measures service downtime as the
unavailability incurred while retraining to erase data
\cite{zhang2025}---but that is an availability notion (the service is
offline), whereas we measure time served online yet below a quality
threshold. We report the detection delay in raw
virtual seconds; the normalized delay (NDT) defined by the benchmark
\cite{cerqueira2026} is left as future work.

\section{Problem Statement}
We consider synchronous FL with $C$ clients and virtual time $v$, the
abstract clock of our simulator. A model is trained on clean data for $R$
rounds, then enters production at time $v_0$. Production data is served in
monitoring ticks of $\Delta t$ seconds, one unlabeled batch per client per
tick.

\textbf{Gradual drift model.} A corruption applied to inputs $X$ (labels
preserved) is injected with a mixture fraction $f(v)$ that grows linearly
from zero to one over $N$ ticks:
\begin{equation}
f(v) = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{v-v_0}{N\,\Delta t}\right),
\quad v\ge v_0 .
\label{eq:ramp}
\end{equation}
The ramp duration $N\Delta t$ is the drift width; its slope $1/N$ per tick
is the independent variable of our study. Here $N\in\{5,10,20\}$ ($50$,
$100$, $200$\,s at $\Delta t=10$\,s).

\textbf{Quality threshold and downtime.} Let $a(v)$ be the accuracy of the
served model on a test set mixed at the current fraction $f(v)$. The quality
threshold $\tau$ defines the service state: the model is unavailable when
$a(v)<\tau$. The downtime over a fixed horizon $[v_0,v_0+H]$ is
\begin{equation}
D \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{m} (v_{i+1}-v_i)\cdot
\mathbf{1}\big[a(v_i)<\tau\big] ,
\label{eq:downtime}
\end{equation}
where $v_1,\dots,v_m$ are the evaluation instants and $v_{m+1}=v_0+H$: the
stepwise integral uses the last known quality, without interpolation, and
includes the final interval up to the horizon (always an evaluation
instant). The evaluation instants
comprise tick boundaries, the retraining completion instant, and the
horizon; the post-retraining grid is phase-shifted by the round duration.

\textbf{Paired evaluation.} The reactive policy is compared with a baseline
that observes the same stream from the same checkpoint and never retrains.
Both arms share the same random state, production start, and horizon $H$;
the baseline records the moment the policy \emph{would} have triggered as a
counterfactual. The comparison attributes the downtime difference to the
retraining action within this paired design.

\section{Proposed Method}
\subsection{Local uncertainty detection (UDD per client)}
Each client $c$ runs a local detector over the shared global model:
(1) the model is set to evaluation mode with dropout layers re-enabled;
(2) $T=5$ stochastic forwards produce class probabilities;
(3) the mean Shannon entropy $H=-\sum_k p_k\log p_k$ over the batch is
computed;
(4) the scalar feeds a per-client ADWIN with $\delta=0.002$
\cite{bifet2007,baier2023}.
Our ADWIN implementation only tests windows of at least 20 observations
(the reference implementation \cite{bifet2007} uses a bucket structure
with smaller minima), because we feed one batch-mean entropy per tick per
client rather than a per-instance stream. Each detector therefore receives
20 warm-up ticks on clean data before production starts. The warm-up
calibrates the detectors but does not feed the collective policy.
The detector never mutates the model: training modes are restored
after each update, and only the inputs $X$ are observed.

\subsection{Collective quorum policy}
At each production tick, the server receives the per-client alarm flags and
maintains a sliding window of the last two ticks. The flagged fraction is
the number of \emph{distinct} clients flagged in the window divided by the
number of clients. When the fraction reaches the quorum $\theta=0.30$
(3 of 10 clients), the server triggers, and a latch prevents any further
decision in the episode. In the baseline arm the same computation runs with
\texttt{record\_action=false} and no retraining happens. A single isolated
alarm (a possible local false positive under continuous monitoring
\cite{poenaru2022}) is not enough to trigger.

\subsection{Reactive retraining on the current mixture}
When the policy triggers at time $t_d$, each client's training dataset is
replaced by a mixture with corrupted fraction $f(t_d)$, built with a
deterministic per-client permutation, and a single synchronous FL round is
executed with all clients. Each client holds 5,000 training images (an IID
split of the 50,000-image training set), so the retraining set is the
client's 5,000-image set mixed at $f(t_d)$. The retraining learns the
current distribution and does not predict the future of the ramp. The
budget is explicit (one round); if the required
virtual time would exceed the remaining horizon, the decision is recorded
and the action skipped (this did not occur in our grid). A retraining round
consumes about 8\,s of virtual time (one server timeout); in every detected
episode the accuracy at the round's completion already meets $\tau$, so the
time-to-recovery equals the retraining duration.

\subsection{Evaluation on the current mixture}
The accuracy series is measured on the full test set (10,000 images) mixed
at the current fraction $f(v)$: at production start the test is clean
(fraction zero), and it becomes fully corrupted only when the ramp
completes; a fully corrupted test would overstate the degradation during
the ramp. Mixing uses a deterministic permutation seeded per episode, so
both arms are evaluated on the identical tensor at every instant.

\subsection{Severity calibration}
Each corruption is calibrated on a clean checkpoint: all severities $1$--$5$
are evaluated on the corrupted test set, and the smallest severity whose
accuracy falls strictly inside $(0.25, 0.50)$ is selected. The lower bound
keeps the model well above chance (0.10 for ten classes), so recovery is
achievable with a small budget; the upper bound guarantees the
corruption pushes the service below $\tau$ (so downtime exists to be
avoided).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Dataset and corruptions}
We use CIFAR-10 with an IID partition across 10 clients and the full 10
classes in all phases. Four visual corruptions are implemented directly on
tensors---Gaussian noise, frosted-glass blur, motion blur, and fog---with
integer severities $1$--$5$, per-seed generators, and preserved shapes and
labels. They are \emph{inspired by}, not a reproduction of, the CIFAR-10-C
benchmark \cite{hendrycks2019}; their internal parameters are
project-specific: Gaussian noise adds perturbations with
per-severity standard deviations of 0.08--0.24; frosted-glass blur permutes
pixels within a $3\times3$ neighborhood and blends with a Gaussian blur
(strength 0.2--1.0); motion blur convolves with a diagonal kernel of width
5--15; fog blends the image with a four-octave fractal noise map. The
complete parameterization accompanies the released code.

\section{Results}
We report group means over the five seeds. We test two hypotheses: H1, the reactive policy reduces downtime relative
to the no-retraining baseline; H2, the detection delay grows with the ramp
duration. Given $n=5$ per
group, inferential claims are limited to a pooled paired comparison; the
per-group results are reported descriptively with dispersion. The primary
measurements are the detection delay (production start to trigger), the
mixture fraction at the trigger, and the downtime; secondary metrics are
the clean retention (final clean accuracy minus pre-drift clean accuracy)
and the final corrupted accuracy. Full per-seed values accompany the
released artifacts.

\subsection{Detection: 59 of 60 episodes}
Detection succeeded in 59 of the 60 episodes (14 of 15 for Gaussian noise).
The only failure is Gaussian noise, 100\,s ramp, seed 42, analyzed in
Section VII-A.

delay grows with the ramp duration for the three visual corruptions,
consistent with the ADWIN analysis of gradual change with constant slope
\cite{bifet2007}; Gaussian noise does not follow this trend and shows much
larger dispersion (per-seed std of 71--104\,s).

\subsection{Collective-quorum analysis}
Whenever a decision is taken, \emph{all} 10 clients raise an alarm at some
point of the episode (59 of 59): the local detectors are individually
sensitive to the gradual drift. The trigger fires exactly when
the quorum is met at its minimum (0.30, three distinct clients in the
two-tick window) in 21 of the 59 episodes; Gaussian noise is the extreme
case, firing at the minimum in all detected episodes of the 100\,s ramp.
The long ramp spreads the alarms in time: the flagged fraction at the
decision drops with the ramp duration (fog 0.88$\rightarrow$0.42, frosted
0.90$\rightarrow$0.56, motion 0.66$\rightarrow$0.54), because alarms arrive
at more scattered ticks and co-occur later, at a smaller fraction of the
ramp.
These properties reflect the global, simultaneous drift of our scenario;
staggered drifts, which motivate the quorum in \cite{jothimurugesan2023},
are not exercised here.

Two findings deserve emphasis. First, \emph{long ramps preserve
the clean-set accuracy} (Table~\ref{tab:retention}): with the 200\,s ramp
the retraining set keeps roughly 30\% of clean data and the clean-accuracy
loss drops to 1--2 points (versus 9--21 on short ramps) for fog,
frosted-glass, and motion blur; Gaussian noise does not benefit: its
trigger occurs after the ramp (fraction 0.97). Second, \emph{the recovery
is not temporary}: no episode shows the accuracy re-crossing $\tau$ within
the fixed horizon; the retrained model generalizes to the fully corrupted
regime. In 9 of the 20 episodes with the 200\,s ramp (all five fog runs,
three frosted runs, one motion run) the accuracy never falls below $\tau$.
Group-mean final corrupted accuracy
ranges from 0.60 to 0.71 for the reactive arm (excluding the failed
episode at 0.33) and 0.35--0.48 for the baseline
(Fig.~\ref{fig:motion}).

\section{Discussion}
Monitoring is not free, but it does not dominate the reactive budget:
over an episode the $T=5$ detector costs about 6.8\,TFLOPs (4{,}000
batch-32 forwards, 50 per tick across the ten clients) against about
8.0\,TFLOPs for the single retraining round, a ratio of 0.85--0.96$\times$
depending on the accounting---the same order of magnitude, and roughly 47\%
of the reactive compute. On the measured device (Apple M5, PyTorch 2.12.1)
one detector update takes about 12.7\,ms per client; the per-tick monitor
across the ten clients occupies about 0.13\,s, 1.3\% of each 10\,s tick.
The forward and FLOP counts refer to the virtual 80-tick episode; the
12.7\,ms figure is measured wall-clock. Had we kept the published UDD
setting $T=50$, monitoring would exceed the retraining round by roughly
$9\times$, which is
exactly why $T=5$ was chosen as a cost decision; the accuracy sensitivity
to $T$ is left as future work.

The downtime conclusions are robust to a $10\times$ longer round:
recomputing downtime post hoc from the recorded series---charging the
pre-retrain accuracy over $[t_d,t_d+d]$, the first post-retrain record
thereafter, with the controller's budget rule applied---an 80\,s
communication-dominated round still cuts the mean
downtime from 507.5\,s (baseline) to 122.2\,s (76\%); the per-group
ranking, the zero-downtime fog case at the 200\,s ramp, and the
detection-limited Gaussian behaviour are unchanged, the round's extra
cost being exactly the pre-retrain interval it extends (zero when the
detector fires early). At $d=800$\,s the round no longer fits the 600\,s
production horizon and the budget rule would skip retraining in all 59
triggered episodes, degenerating the agent to the baseline by policy
rather than by training dynamics; the turning point is the smallest
trigger-to-horizon slack, about 140\,s in this grid.

\subsection{Gaussian noise is the limiting scenario}
Gaussian noise is the only scenario with incomplete detection (14 of 15
episodes). With the 100\,s ramp and seed 42,
the policy never triggers: only four of the ten clients raise an alarm
during the whole episode, never three within the same two-tick window
(maximum flagged fraction 0.20, at sparse ticks), so the quorum is never
met; the other four seeds of the group detect with delays of 120--210\,s.
Because the window and the quorum are identical across seeds, the failure
is best explained as a per-client detector miss on this seed; our design
cannot isolate an interaction among the signal, the window, and the
quorum. Without a decision, the reactive arm degenerates exactly to the
baseline (both arms end with the same downtime, 540\,s). With the 200\,s
ramp the
delays spread between 180 and 460\,s (std 104\,s), yet the reactive arm
still cuts the baseline downtime by 75\% (112 vs.\ 448\,s), consistent with the
risk registered for ADWIN-style detectors under long, gentle drifts
\cite{poenaru2022}. Fig.~\ref{fig:gaussian} shows the failed seed inside
its group.

\subsection{Why the long ramp behaves better}
For the visual corruptions the 200\,s ramp concentrates three favorable
effects. (i) The trigger occurs mid-ramp, at fractions 0.68--0.70, where
the accuracy at the decision is 0.57 for fog (still above $\tau$, hence
zero downtime), 0.51 for frosted-glass, and 0.48 for motion blur. (ii)
The retraining set mixes about 30\% clean data, reducing
catastrophic forgetting. (iii) The model retrained at fraction
$\approx$0.7 generalizes to the fully corrupted regime, so the recovery
holds until the horizon. Short ramps behave like the abrupt regime:
detection completes after the ramp and the retraining set is almost fully
corrupted. Gaussian noise triggers later (fraction 0.97) and does not
enjoy effects (i) and (ii).

\subsection{Limitations}
Our results are bounded by the following declared limitations. (i) Five
seeds per group limit the precision of the dispersion estimate. (ii) The
model is a small CNN of about 2.2 million parameters; absolute accuracies
under corruption are modest, as expected for small architectures
\cite{hendrycks2019}. (iii) The scenario assumes a single drift, one
retraining round, an IID partition, uniform speed, and a fixed
horizon; the paired comparison isolates the retraining action within these
conditions. (iv) The fraction at the trigger is informative only in the
long-ramp regime; the trigger occurs after the ramp completes for short
ramps. (v) The published UDD uses $T=50$ forwards; our $T=5$ is a cost
decision without published equivalence, and $\delta=0.002$ is the fallback
of the reference implementation rather than a calibrated
value \cite{baier2023,poenaru2022}. (vi) Downtime is sampled at tick
granularity plus the round-completion instant; the true crossing of
$\tau$ can fall between ticks. (vii) Episodes ran on a CUDA GPU
(PyTorch 2.5.1, CUDA 12.1) with non-deterministic algorithms; bit-identical
reproduction was not confirmed, although each pair is validated to share an
identical checkpoint, stream, and horizon. (viii) The
severities were calibrated on a clean checkpoint from a separate training
run; the calibration digest differs from the episode
checkpoints, and severities are not re-validated per seed. (ix)
The reported contrasts depend on $\tau=0.5$ and the calibrated
severities; we report descriptive group means over 12 groups without
multiple-comparison correction. (x) The evaluation compares the
reactive policy only with a no-retraining baseline; other comparators are
future work, so our claims concern the value of retraining reactively
rather than never, not the superiority of this detection mechanism over
other triggers. (xi)
No drift-free control episodes were run, so the detector's false-positive
rate under continuous monitoring is unmeasured; sensitivity to $\tau$, the
quorum, and the window is future work.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We studied reactive retraining in synchronous federated learning across the
abrupt-to-gradual drift continuum, evaluating
a reactive policy---per-client label-free UDD detection, a server-side
quorum over a two-tick window, and a single retraining round on the current
mixture---against a paired no-retraining baseline over 60 episodes with
service downtime as the primary metric. Detection succeeded in 59 of 60
episodes; the delay grew with the ramp duration for the visual corruptions;
the reactive arm reduced the mean downtime from 408--562\,s to 0--178\,s,
reaching zero at $\tau=0.5$ in the fog scenario; and long ramps preserved
clean accuracy for the visual corruptions. Short ramps behaved like the
abrupt regime. The single detection failure (Gaussian noise, one seed) is
a per-client detector miss whose interaction with the window-quorum policy
our design cannot isolate.

Future work includes: scheduled-retraining, first-alarm, centralized-
detector, and oracle baselines to isolate the contribution of the detection
mechanism; drift-free control episodes to measure false positives; a
sensitivity sweep over $\tau$ and the calibration bounds; a quorum
ablation, including staggered drift; more seeds and a formal dispersion
analysis; recurrent drift; non-IID partitions and heterogeneous client
speeds; incremental drift; larger models; and publishing the protocol as a
reusable benchmark for FL-under-drift evaluation
\cite{cerqueira2026,mahdi2025}.
